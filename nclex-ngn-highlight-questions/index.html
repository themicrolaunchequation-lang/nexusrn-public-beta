NCLEX NGN Highlight Questions Explained

Highlight questions test whether you can identify the exact words in the client scenario that matter most. Your job is not to mark everything abnormal. Your job is to select the evidence that answers the task.

Back to NGN Study Hub Review Case Study Questions

A highlight item gives you text and asks you to select specific words, phrases, or sentences. The challenge is precision. Broad highlighting feels safe, but the NCLEX rewards the evidence that matches the question.

The learner trap: Highlighting every abnormal phrase is not clinical judgment. It is panic with a yellow marker.

What is an NCLEX NGN highlight question?

In a highlight question, the answer is inside the text. You may be asked to select a cue, a concerning statement, a priority assessment finding, or evidence that an intervention worked.

Not every word is selectable on the actual exam interface. The important study skill is learning how to separate useful evidence from distracting background information.

The highlight-question method

  1. Read the task first. Decide whether the question wants cues, risks, priority findings, actions, or outcomes.
  2. Find the client problem. Do not highlight until you know what clinical issue is being tested.
  3. Mark evidence, not decoration. Highlight text that directly proves the answer.
  4. Avoid over-highlighting. More selected text is not automatically better.
  5. Reread the stem. Make sure the highlighted phrase answers the exact question asked.

Original sample highlight question

This original public sample is simplified for learning. It is not copied from NCLEX, NCSBN, or any protected NexusRN question bank.

Client note

A 64-year-old client is recovering after abdominal surgery. The client states, “My pain suddenly became much worse” and appears restless. The abdomen is firm and distended. Blood pressure is 88/54 mm Hg, heart rate is 124/min, and the dressing has a small amount of old drainage.

Task

Select the findings that require immediate follow-up.

Best highlighted evidence

  • “My pain suddenly became much worse”
  • 88/54 mm Hg
  • 124/min
  • Firm and distended abdomen

The old drainage may matter, but the most urgent pattern is possible internal bleeding or deterioration: sudden worsening pain, hypotension, tachycardia, restlessness, and abdominal distention.

Why highlight items test clinical judgment

Recognize cues

You must notice which details are abnormal, new, or unsafe.

Analyze cues

You must connect the highlighted details into a clinical pattern.

Prioritize hypotheses

You must decide which explanation creates the greatest risk.

Evaluate outcomes

You may need to select text showing whether the client improved or worsened.

Common mistakes on highlight questions

  • Over-highlighting: selecting every abnormal word instead of the exact requested evidence.
  • Ignoring the task: highlighting symptoms when the question asks for outcome evidence.
  • Missing trend language: words like sudden, new, worsening, decreased, or unresolved often matter.
  • Choosing background details: age and history can matter, but the question may need current deterioration.
  • Highlighting diagnosis labels: the safer answer is often the cue that proves the diagnosis or risk.

Practice drill

Before you highlight, ask:

  1. What kind of evidence is the question asking for?
  2. Which phrase proves the current client risk?
  3. Which detail sounds important but does not answer this task?
  4. Could I defend this highlight using the chart?
